About this tool

The Fake QR Code Checker was built by CyberForge, a student cybersecurity team, to help everyday people check a QR code before opening it. It runs entirely in your browser — no server, no data collection.

Everything runs locally. The QR image is decoded using jsQR in your browser. The decoded text is checked against a set of heuristics. Nothing is sent to a server at any point.

The tool is free to use for personal and educational purposes. It is not a replacement for antivirus software or professional security tooling.

How it works

  1. 1
    Provide the QR code Upload an image file, point your camera at the code, or paste the link directly if you already have it.
  2. 2
    Decode in browser jsQR reads the pixel data and extracts the encoded text — entirely on your device. The image is never sent anywhere.
  3. 3
    Run heuristic checks The decoded text is checked against 11 patterns associated with phishing and malicious links. Each triggered pattern adds points to a risk score.
  4. 4
    Show the verdict The address is broken into labelled parts. Any flagged part is highlighted with the specific reason attached. The verdict appears alongside it.

The three verdicts

✓ No warning signs found ⚠ Suspicious ✕ Dangerous

No warning signs found means none of the checked patterns were triggered. It is not a guarantee the link is safe — new threats appear constantly and heuristics have limits.

Suspicious (score 20–44) means one or more low-to-medium signals were found. The link may be legitimate, but it is worth a closer look before opening it.

Dangerous (score 45+) means a combination of strong signals was found. This strongly suggests a phishing attempt or malicious destination. Do not open the link.

The verdict is shaped by a weighted score, not a single yes/no flag. A link can trigger a low-weight signal (like being over 100 characters) without reaching Suspicious. A single high-weight signal (like a brand name appearing in a domain that is not that brand's real domain, +30 points) can push a link to Dangerous on its own if other factors are also present.

Limitations

This tool checks patterns in the address itself. It does not:

  • Visit or fetch the destination URL in any way
  • Check the destination against a live blocklist or threat database
  • Analyse the content of the linked page
  • Follow redirects to find the final destination of a shortened URL
  • Detect newly registered phishing domains not yet flagged by any service

For deeper analysis, use a service like VirusTotal or urlscan.io. These send the URL to external servers, which is a trade-off to be aware of.

How to spot a fake QR code

  • Sticker on top of the real code. Look closely at payment QR codes on counters and parking meters. A sticker pasted over the original is the most common physical attack.
  • The address does not match the place. A coffee shop should link to a coffee shop domain, not "pay-now-secure.xyz". Read the destination before you open it.
  • Payment requests hidden in a link. Some QR codes encode a UPI or payment link directly. Read the recipient and amount carefully before confirming.
  • Urgency in the message. "Scan now to claim your prize." "Your parcel is held." Urgency is a manipulation tactic, not a reason to hurry.
  • Bank and government websites. Type the address yourself. Never rely on a QR code for your banking app, tax portal, or any login page that matters.
